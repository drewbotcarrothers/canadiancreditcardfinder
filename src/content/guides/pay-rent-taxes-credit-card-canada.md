**Rent and taxes are often a household's largest bills, and most landlords and the Canada Revenue Agency (CRA) don't accept credit cards directly. Third-party services will charge your card and pay the bill for you, for a fee usually in the 1.75% to 2.5% range. Whether that's worth it comes down to one comparison: is the value of the rewards you earn bigger than the fee, after accounting for how your card codes the transaction?** For many cards, the answer is no. For some situations, such as meeting a welcome bonus spending requirement, it can be yes.

This guide explains how these payments work, how to do the math, and the risks.

## Why you can't just pay with a card

Accepting a credit card costs the payee a fee. Most landlords prefer e-Transfer, cheques or pre-authorized debit for that reason.

The CRA's [payment options page](https://www.canada.ca/en/revenue-agency/services/payments/payments-cra/individual-payments/make-payment.html) is explicit: its own My Payment service takes debit cards only, not credit cards. For credit card payments, the CRA lists third-party service providers, currently **PaySimply** and **Plastiq**, that accept your card and send the money to the CRA on your behalf. The CRA notes that service fees are charged by the provider and that it doesn't endorse them.

## How payment services work

The model is the same for rent and taxes:

1. You register the payee: your landlord's e-Transfer details, a bill payment account, or the CRA.
2. The service charges your credit card the amount plus its fee.
3. It sends the money to the payee by e-Transfer, bill payment, deposit or pre-authorized debit.

For example, [Chexy](https://chexy.co/), a Canadian service, says it charges a flat 1.75% per payment on Canadian-issued cards, charges your card four business days before the payment is due, and pays using the method your payee prefers. It handles rent, utilities, taxes and other bills. Fees and features change, so check the service's current pricing.

Some property management companies run their own portals that take cards, usually with a fee. Merchants can pass on credit card surcharges of up to 2.4% in most of Canada (not in Quebec), according to the [Financial Consumer Agency of Canada](https://www.canada.ca/en/financial-consumer-agency/services/credit-cards/credit-card-work.html), and must disclose them.

## The break-even math

Start with the fee and compare it with your card's return on that transaction.

**Example: $2,000 rent at a 1.75% fee**

- Fee: $2,000 × 1.75% = **$35**
- Card earning 1% cash back: $20 back. **You lose $15.**
- Card earning 2% cash back: $40 back. **You gain $5.**
- Points card earning 1 point per $1, with points worth 2 cents each when redeemed well: 2,000 points ≈ $40. **You gain about $5**, if you actually redeem at that value.

Over a year, the 2% card nets about $60 on $24,000 of rent. That's real, but small, and it disappears with a single month of interest.

A few cards earn 2% or more on everyday purchases. The [SimplyCash Preferred Card from American Express](/card/simplycash-preferred-card-from-american-express/), for instance, earns 2% on purchases outside its bonus categories, though it carries an annual fee, and you'd need a service that accepts Amex. For points cards like the [RBC Avion Visa Infinite](/card/rbc-avion-visa-infinite/), the value depends on how you redeem. Our [points value guide](/guides/credit-card-points-value-canada/) explains how to estimate a point's worth.

## Check how your card codes the payment

The earn rate you see in a card's marketing may not apply. Payment services are usually classified as their own merchant type, so:

- **Bonus categories won't apply.** You won't earn a grocery or dining multiplier on rent.
- **Some cards earn only the base rate** on payments made through third-party payment services. The American Express Cobalt Card's terms, for example, say purchases through third-party payment services earn the base 1 point per dollar.
- **Some issuers exclude certain payments** from rewards or treat them as cash-like transactions. BMO's Pay with Points terms, for example, exclude tax payments as an eligible category for that redemption.

Read your card's rewards terms, or make one small payment and check how it posts before you commit to a large one.

## When it can make sense

**Meeting a welcome bonus requirement.** If a new card needs $5,000 in spending in three months and your normal spending won't get there, putting a month's rent or a tax instalment on it can unlock a bonus worth hundreds of dollars. The fee is then a small cost against a large reward. Confirm that payments through the service count as eligible purchases under the offer terms.

**A high-earning card.** If your card earns well above the fee on every purchase, the math can work, especially for points you value highly.

**Cash flow timing.** Paying by card buys you time until your statement is due. That only helps if you pay the statement in full; it's not a substitute for having the money.

## When it doesn't

- **You might carry the balance.** At a typical purchase rate around 20%, one month of interest on $2,000 is roughly $33, which wipes out the rewards and then some. Our [guide to interest, grace periods and minimum payments](/guides/credit-card-interest-grace-period-minimum-payment-canada/) explains how it adds up.
- **Your card earns less than the fee,** which is true of many no-fee cards earning 0.5% to 1%.
- **The payment would push up your utilization** right before you apply for a mortgage or car loan. A large statement balance can affect your credit score until you pay it. See [how credit scores work](/guides/how-credit-scores-work-canada/).
- **The timing is tight.** A payment service adds processing days.

## Paying the CRA: timing matters

If you pay taxes through PaySimply or Plastiq, remember that the CRA considers the payment made when the CRA receives it, not when you charge your card. Allow several business days, and check the provider's processing times. A late tax payment can mean CRA interest and penalties that dwarf any rewards. For instalment and balance-owing deadlines, follow the CRA's guidance, and pay early.

Also confirm whether your issuer treats the payment as a purchase or something else, and whether the provider's fee itself earns rewards.

## Safety and practical tips

- Use services the CRA lists or that are well established, and check that the website address is correct before entering card details. Watch for CRA scam messages; the CRA's page links to guidance on avoiding them.
- Tell your landlord how payments will arrive, especially if the sender name will be the payment service rather than you.
- Keep receipts from the service as proof of payment.
- Calendar the processing lead time so rent is never late.
- Don't split a payment across cards in ways that break a lease's payment terms.

## Building credit with rent

Some services offer rent reporting to a credit bureau as an add-on. Chexy, for example, offers a paid Credit Builder feature that reports rent payments to Equifax. Whether that's worth paying for depends on how thin your credit file is. For most people, a credit card paid in full every month builds credit without an extra fee. ## FAQ

**Can I pay my CRA taxes with a credit card?**
Not directly. The CRA lists PaySimply and Plastiq as third-party providers that accept credit cards, for a fee. CRA's own My Payment service takes debit only.

**Does paying rent with a credit card earn rewards?**
Usually, but often at your card's base rate. Some cards earn less, or nothing, on certain payment types. Check your card's terms.

**Is the fee tax-deductible?**
For most individuals paying personal rent or personal income tax, generally no. If the payment relates to a business, ask an accountant.

**Is it a cash advance?**
Normally a payment service transaction is processed as a purchase, but issuers set their own rules. If it posts as a cash advance, interest starts immediately and there's no grace period.

**Will my landlord know I paid by credit card?**
They may see the payment come from the service. The landlord doesn't pay the fee; you do.
