**Credit card travel insurance can save you thousands, but only if your trip fits inside its limits. The key numbers are the trip length covered (often 15 to 25 days), the age at which coverage shrinks (usually 65), the dollar caps on trip cancellation, and the conditions around pre-existing medical issues.** Many travellers find out about those limits at the worst possible moment.

This guide explains each type of coverage, shows real limits from Canadian cards, and lists what to check before you rely on a card instead of buying a separate policy. It's general information, not insurance advice. The certificate of insurance for your card is the document that decides any claim.

## Why you need travel medical coverage at all

Provincial health plans pay very little outside Canada. Ontario's government, for example, says [OHIP pays](https://www.ontario.ca/page/ohip-coverage-while-outside-canada) the lesser of the hospital's bill or up to $50 a day for emergency outpatient services abroad, and up to $400 a day for emergency inpatient care in places such as an intensive care unit. The province recommends buying private health insurance before leaving. A few days in a U.S. hospital can cost far more than that, so the gap is real. Other provinces have similar limits; check yours before you travel.

## The main types of coverage

Premium cards usually bundle several separate insurance benefits. Each has its own rules.

**Emergency travel medical.** Pays for unexpected, emergency medical care while you're away: hospital stays, doctors, ambulance, sometimes medical evacuation home. It's the most important benefit, and the one with the tightest limits on trip length and age.

**Trip cancellation.** Reimburses non-refundable prepaid costs if you have to cancel before you leave for a covered reason, such as a serious illness or the death of a family member.

**Trip interruption.** Covers extra costs and unused prepaid portions if a covered reason cuts your trip short or delays your return.

**Flight delay and baggage.** Pays for reasonable essentials (meals, a hotel, clothes, toiletries) after a delay of a set number of hours, or if your bags are delayed or lost.

**Rental car collision/loss damage.** Covers damage to or theft of a rental car when you decline the rental company's collision damage waiver. It's not liability insurance.

Sources: [TD's card page](https://www.td.com/ca/en/personal-banking/products/credit-cards/aeroplan/aeroplan-visa-infinite-card), RBC's Avion Visa Infinite certificate of insurance, Scotiabank's Passport Visa Infinite certificate summary, and [Amex's Cobalt page](https://www.americanexpress.com/en-ca/credit-cards/cobalt-card/). Limits change, so confirm before every trip.

Two things stand out. First, the day count is usually the **first** X days of a trip, starting on your departure date. Second, coverage can drop sharply at 65, or end entirely on some cards. A 66-year-old with a TD Aeroplan Visa Infinite gets four days of coverage, not 21.

## What happens if your trip is longer than the limit

Read this part of your certificate closely. A card that covers the first 15 days doesn't automatically cover days 16 to 30. Depending on the policy, a longer trip may be covered only for the first 15 days, or may need top-up coverage bought before you leave to be insured at all for the extra days.

Most issuers sell a **top-up** that extends medical coverage for the extra days of a specific trip. TD and RBC both mention top-ups on their card pages and certificates. Buy it before you leave, from the same insurer if possible, so there's no gap between policies.

## Pre-existing conditions and "stability"

Travel medical insurance is designed for sudden, unexpected emergencies. Most certificates exclude claims related to a medical condition that wasn't **stable** for a set period before your departure.

RBC's Avion Visa Infinite certificate, for example, uses a 90-day stability period before departure for travellers under 75, and defines "stable" in detail: no new symptoms, no change in treatment or medication, no new test results showing deterioration, and no pending tests or referrals, among other points. A change in medication dosage in the weeks before your trip can matter, even if you feel fine.

If you have an ongoing condition, read the definitions in your certificate, and call the insurer before you go if anything is unclear. A separate policy with a medical questionnaire may be a better fit.

## Who is covered

Certificates define "insured persons" carefully. Typically that includes the primary cardholder, their spouse and dependent children, sometimes whether or not they travel together. Some cards cover additional cardholders as well. Definitions of "spouse" and "dependent child" (age limits, full-time student status) vary, so check them.

## Do you need to pay for the trip with the card?

It depends on the benefit:

- **Trip cancellation and interruption** generally require that you charged the trip, or redeemed the card's points for it. If you paid cash, there's usually nothing to claim.
- **Rental car coverage** requires that you charge the full rental to the card and decline the rental company's damage waiver.
- **Travel medical** is handled differently from card to card. On some cards it applies because you hold the card; on others you must have charged some or all of the trip.

When in doubt, put the trip on the card that provides the coverage. If you split costs across cards or use points from another program, read the certificate's wording on how the trip must be paid for.

## Trip cancellation limits

Cancellation caps are usually per person, with an overall maximum. TD's Aeroplan Visa Infinite page lists up to $1,500 per insured person, to a maximum of $5,000 for all insured persons, and trip interruption up to $5,000 per person and $25,000 in total. RBC's Avion Visa Infinite certificate has a similar $1,500 per person and $5,000 overall cancellation limit.

For an expensive trip, such as a family cruise or a tour costing $8,000 a person, card cancellation coverage may fall far short. Some issuers let you buy extra cancellation coverage for the gap.

Covered reasons are also limited. Changing your mind, work commitments and fear of travel are usually not covered.

## How to make a claim

1. **Call the emergency assistance number first.** For medical emergencies, most certificates require you or someone acting for you to contact the assistance provider before treatment, or as soon as reasonably possible. Not calling can reduce what's paid. Save the number in your phone before you leave.
2. **Keep everything.** Receipts, medical reports, boarding passes, the airline's delay confirmation, cancellation invoices and proof of payment.
3. **Expect coordination with other insurance.** Card coverage often pays after your provincial plan and any group benefits through work.
4. **File within the deadline.** Certificates set time limits for notifying the insurer and sending documents.

## A pre-trip checklist

- Find your card's certificate of insurance (usually a PDF on the issuer's site) and the benefits guide.
- Count your trip days, including your departure and return dates, and compare them with the coverage limit.
- Check the age limits for everyone travelling.
- Check the stability period if anyone has a medical condition.
- Make sure the trip was charged to the card, or redeemed with its points, in the way the certificate requires.
- Buy a top-up before you leave if the trip is longer than the coverage.
- Save the assistance phone number and policy number somewhere you can reach offline.

TD also offers an online credit card travel insurance verification tool, and other issuers let you call to confirm coverage before a trip.

## FAQ

**Is credit card travel insurance as good as a standalone policy?**
For short trips by healthy travellers under 65, a premium card's coverage can be very solid. For long trips, older travellers or anyone with a medical condition, a standalone policy may fit better.

**Does a no-fee card include travel medical insurance?**
Some do, but usually with shorter trip lengths and lower limits. Many no-fee cards include none. Check the benefits guide.

**Does coverage apply to trips within Canada?**
Travel medical usually applies outside your home province, but certificates vary. Trip cancellation can apply to domestic trips if you meet the requirements.

**Do I lose coverage if I cancel or switch my card?**
Yes. Insurance belongs to the card, so a product switch or cancellation ends it. Settle any claims first. See our [product switching guide](/guides/product-switch-vs-cancel-credit-card-canada/).

**Does travel insurance cover foreign transaction fees or exchange rates?**
No. For that, see our [foreign transaction fees guide](/guides/foreign-transaction-fees-canada/).
