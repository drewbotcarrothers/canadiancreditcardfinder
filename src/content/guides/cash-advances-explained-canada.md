**A cash advance is when you use a credit card to get cash or something that works like cash. Unlike purchases, cash advances don't get an interest-free grace period: interest starts the day the transaction is made, and the rate is usually higher than your purchase rate.** Many cards also charge a fee on top.

## What counts as a cash advance

The obvious one is taking cash out of an ATM with your credit card. But cash advance rules often cover more than that. Scotiabank's card pages, for example, say the cash advance rate "applies to cash advances, balance transfers, and cash-like transactions." Depending on the issuer, that can include:

- ATM withdrawals and over-the-counter cash at a bank
- Balance transfers from another card or loan
- Convenience cheques
- Money orders, foreign currency or traveller's cheques bought with the card
- Some gambling, lottery and casino transactions
- Some person-to-person payment or money transfer services

Each issuer defines "cash-like" in its cardholder agreement, so check yours before you do anything unusual with the card.

## How much does it cost?

There are usually three costs:

1. **A higher interest rate.** Across major Canadian cards, cash advance rates are typically 22% to 24%. Some examples:

| Card | Purchase rate | Cash advance rate |
|---|---|---|
| [TD Rewards Visa](/card/td-rewards-visa-card/) | 21.99% | 22.99% |
| [BMO CashBack Mastercard](/card/bmo-cashback-mastercard/) | 21.99% | 23.99% (21.99% in Quebec) |
| [CIBC Dividend Visa](/card/cibc-dividend-visa-card/) | 21.99% | 22.99% |
| [MBNA True Line Mastercard](/card/mbna-true-line-mastercard/) | 12.99% | 24.99% |

The MBNA True Line is a good reminder that a low purchase rate doesn't always mean a low cash advance rate.

2. **No grace period.** Interest starts on the day of the advance, even if you pay your full statement balance by the due date.

3. **A transaction fee.** Many issuers charge a fee per advance, often a flat amount or a percentage, whichever is higher. ATM operators may add their own fee too.

### A quick example

Say you take a $500 cash advance on a card with a 22.99% cash advance rate and repay it 30 days later. The interest is roughly $500 × 22.99% × 30 ÷ 365, or about **$9.45**, plus any transaction fee. That's for one month. Leave it longer and it keeps growing daily.

## How payments are applied

When you make a payment, anything above your minimum payment is generally applied to the balance with the higher interest rate first, which is often the cash advance. Our guide to [interest, grace periods and minimum payments](/guides/credit-card-interest-grace-period-minimum-payment-canada/) explains the rules.

## Balance transfers are cash advances too

Many balance transfer offers are technically cash advance promotions. For example, the Scotia Momentum Visa's current offer is a 0.99% introductory rate on balance transfers for the first 9 months, with a 2% fee per transfer, and after that the amount moves to the cash advance rate (currently 22.99%). That can save money if you pay it off within the promotional period, but read the terms. See [balance transfers explained](/guides/balance-transfers-explained-canada/).

## Cheaper ways to get cash

- **Use your debit card** at your own bank's ATM.
- **Use an overdraft or line of credit**, which often costs less than a credit card cash advance.
- **Travelling?** A card with no foreign transaction fees for purchases, plus a debit card for cash, is usually cheaper than a credit card cash advance abroad. See [foreign transaction fees](/guides/foreign-transaction-fees-canada/).

## FAQ

**Is there a grace period on cash advances?**
No. Interest starts on the day the cash advance is made.

**Do cash advances earn rewards?**
Generally no. Rewards are earned on eligible purchases, not cash advances or balance transfers.

**Does a cash advance hurt my credit score?**
Not directly, but it adds to your balance and raises your utilization, which can affect your score.
